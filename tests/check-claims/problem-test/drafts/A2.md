**AI in B2B Sales in 2026: Widely Adopted, Unevenly Rewarded**

Two years ago, the question for sales leaders was whether to use AI. In 2026, the question is why some teams are getting results from it while most are still waiting.

**Adoption is no longer the story**

The headline numbers settled a while ago. Salesforce's State of Sales report (2024) found that 81% of sales teams were either experimenting with AI or had fully implemented it. McKinsey's State of AI survey (2025) reported that 88% of organisations use AI regularly in at least one business function, with marketing and sales consistently among the most common.

So almost everyone has it. Far fewer have changed how they sell. The same McKinsey survey found only 23% of organisations were scaling AI agents anywhere in the business, with a further 39% still experimenting. MIT's widely discussed "GenAI Divide" study (2025) went further, claiming 95% of generative AI pilots produced no measurable impact on profit and loss. You can argue with the method, but most revenue leaders will recognise the pattern: plenty of licences, not much lift.

**Where the gains are showing up**

The teams getting value tend to point AI at the same problem: the hours reps spend not selling. Salesforce's research has repeatedly found that reps spend around 70% of their week on non-selling work such as admin, data entry and meeting preparation. That is where AI pays back first.

The evidence is strongest in three areas:

- **Research and preparation.** Account briefs, call preparation and CRM updates that once took an hour now take minutes.
- **Conversation intelligence.** Call analysis that flags deal risk and coaching moments without a manager listening to every recording.
- **Pipeline hygiene and forecasting.** Signals drawn from real activity rather than rep optimism.

In Salesforce's 2024 data, 83% of sales teams using AI reported revenue growth, compared with 66% of teams without it. That is correlation, not proof, but the gap is hard to ignore.

**Companies doing it well**

**Microsoft** is one of the more credible examples because it published results from its own sales organisation. Its internal study of sellers using Copilot reported 9.4% higher revenue per seller and 20% more deals closed among high-usage sellers.

**Salesforce** has been its own first customer for Agentforce, deploying AI agents on inbound lead follow-up and on its help site, and publishing the results. Agreeing with the strategy or not, running your own product at scale before selling it is the right order.

**Gong** has built its business on analysing real sales conversations. Its Gong Labs research, drawn from millions of recorded calls, has reported meaningfully higher revenue per rep among teams that use AI regularly.

**HubSpot** has taken the mid-market route, building prospecting and research agents directly into the CRM so smaller teams get the benefit without a data science function.

What these companies share is that AI sits inside the daily workflow, tied to a metric someone owns. None of them treated it as a side tool.

**What separates the winners**

Three things stand out:

1. **Clean data first.** AI on top of a messy CRM produces confident nonsense faster.
2. **One workflow at a time.** The successful rollouts fixed a single, measurable job before expanding.
3. **Humans stay on the relationship.** Buyers have noticed the flood of AI-written outreach, and reply rates on generic sequences reflect it. The gains come from giving reps more time with buyers, not from more automated messages.

**The outlook**

Gartner has predicted that by 2028, 60% of B2B seller work will be executed through generative AI interfaces, up from under 5% in 2023. Whether or not that lands on schedule, the direction is clear.

The gap in 2026 is not between teams with AI and teams without it. It is between teams that redesigned the work and teams that bought the licence.
